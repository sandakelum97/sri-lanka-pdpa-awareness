# PDPA for Small Businesses – What Do I Need to Know?

*For grocery shops, retail stores, restaurants and cafés, salons, tuition classes, small clinics and pharmacies, online sellers, freelancers and the self-employed.*

You keep customers' numbers in your phone. You have a book of regulars. Maybe a camera over the till. None of that is illegal, and none of it will be. But from **1 January 2027** the law expects you to do a few plain things with that information, and this page lists them. Two warnings first: nothing here is legal advice, and we are not the Data Protection Authority. Everything links to the Act so you can check.

![What the law asks at each step](/img/data-lifecycle.svg)

## 1. Does this law apply to me?

Yes, if you run a business and keep *any* information about identifiable people — customers, students, patients, staff, suppliers — on paper, on a phone, in Excel, in WhatsApp, or in an app. The Act applies to processing "wholly or partly within Sri Lanka" (s.2(1)(a), in force from 1 Jan 2027). The only exemption is information used "purely for personal, domestic or household purposes by an individual" (s.2(3)(a)) — your private phone contacts, not your customer list.

There is no small-business exemption and no turnover threshold. What *does* scale with size is how much you have to do: the Act expressly says your compliance programme should match your "structure, scale, volume and sensitivity" of processing (s.12(1)(b)).

## 2. The words you need

- **Personal data** — anything that identifies a person: name, NIC number, phone, address, photo, CCTV image, account number (s.56).
- **Special categories** — health, religion, ethnicity, political opinion, sexual life, criminal records, biometrics, genetic data, and **anything about a child under 16** (s.56). These need a stronger legal reason (Schedule II).
- **Controller** — you, if you decide why and how the information is used.
- **Processor** — a company that handles the information for you on your instructions: a courier, payroll service, cloud app, SMS gateway.
- **Data subject** — the person the information is about.

## 3. What customer information can I collect, and why?

You may collect what you need for a stated, legitimate purpose (ss.6–7) and you must have a legal reason from Schedule I (s.5). For a small business the reasons that matter are:

| Your situation | Legal reason | Consent needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Taking an order, booking, delivery or appointment | Necessary for the contract — Sch I(b) | No |
| Keeping invoices, tax and employment records | Legal obligation — Sch I(c) | No |
| Running your business sensibly (security, fraud checks, your own customers) | Legitimate interests — Sch I(f), (h) | No, but the customer can object once Part II is in force |
| Sending promotional SMS, WhatsApp, email | Consent — Sch I(a), Sch III, and s.27 | **Yes** (Part IV, not yet in force) |
| Anything about health, religion, or a child | A Schedule II ground — often parental/explicit consent | Usually yes |

Consent means a clear, separate, freely given "yes" that can be withdrawn (Sch III). A pre-ticked box or "by buying you agree" does not count.

## 4. Telephone numbers

Collect a number for a reason (order ready, delivery, reminder) and use it for that. Using it for promotions is a different purpose: get a separate opt-in. See scenario 01 and scenario 11.

## 5. Customer records — paper and digital

- Write down (one page is enough) what you keep, why, where, who can see it, and how long you keep it. This is the start of your "Data Protection Management Programme" (s.12).
- Keep records only as long as needed (s.9). Other laws set minimum periods for invoices and employment records — follow those, then dispose.
- Keep them accurate (s.8).

## 6. Protecting paper records

Legal duty: "appropriate technical and organizational measures" (s.10). For paper that means a lockable cabinet or drawer, not leaving customer books on the counter, and shredding when disposing. *(Good practice, not statute: a sign-out sheet for files; no customer forms in the shop window or bin.)*

## 7. Securing computers and mobile phones

Legal duty: s.10 names access controls and encryption as examples. *(Good practice, not statute: screen lock and PIN on every phone and PC; separate logins per staff member; keep software updated; turn on multi-factor authentication for email and cloud accounts; back up; wipe devices before sale.)*

## 8. Employee information

Your staff are data subjects too. Collect the NIC number where a law requires it (EPF, tax); photocopy the NIC only if you really need the copy. Health certificates are special-category data. Do not rely on "consent" in the employment contract — rely on the contract and legal obligations. Give staff a short written notice (s.11). See scenario 07.

## 9. Handling customer requests

From 1 January 2027 you must *facilitate* customers' rights through your internal procedures (s.12(1)(h)). The rights themselves (access, correction, erasure, objection) and the one-month response rule (s.17, as amended) are in **Part II, which is not yet in force**. Practical approach: answer reasonable requests now anyway; keep a simple log of requests and what you did.

## 10. CCTV

Signs at the entrance (s.11); a fixed overwrite period (s.9); restricted viewing (s.10). Do not post footage of customers online. Larger systems need an impact assessment before switching on (s.24(1)(b)); whether a two-camera shop does is an open question — the form is not yet prescribed. See scenario 10.

## 11. WhatsApp and messaging apps

Using WhatsApp for orders is processing, and because the servers are abroad it is a **cross-border data flow** (s.26). It is not banned. Keep business chats on a business number, do not forward customer details to personal groups, and delete order chats when done. For *promotional* broadcasts, consent and an opt-out are required once s.27 is in force. See scenarios 11 and 12.

## 12. Cloud applications

Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Dropbox, Shopify, Zoho, foreign POS and accounting apps: allowed, but (a) the provider is your processor — keep its data-processing terms (s.21); (b) mention overseas storage in your notice (Sch V item 1(h)); (c) the Authority will specify the safeguard instruments you must use by directive (s.26(2)) — not yet issued. See scenario 12.

## 13. Disposing of old records

Shred paper; securely delete files; wipe or destroy old phones and hard drives. Keep a note of what was disposed of and when (it is part of showing compliance under s.12).

## 14. If you suspect a data breach

![What to do when data is lost](/img/breach-steps.svg)

A lost phone, a stolen laptop, a hacked email account, a customer list sent to the wrong person — these are "personal data breaches" (s.56). From 1 January 2027 you must notify the Data Protection Authority "in such form, manner and within such period of time as may be determined by rules" (s.23) — **the rules do not exist yet, so there is no legal deadline today.** The DPA's 2024 draft proposed 72 hours; it is a proposal. Do now: contain it (change passwords, remote-wipe), record what happened, tell affected customers if they could be harmed, and watch dpa.gov.lk for the rules.

## 15. Do I need a Data Protection Officer?

Almost certainly not. Mandatory DPOs are for Ministries and government departments (s.20(1)(a)). Private businesses need one only for large-scale monitoring or large-scale sensitive-data processing "on a scale and magnitude as may be prescribed" — nothing has been prescribed. The DPA's 2024 draft suggested a 25,000-data-subject indicator; it is a draft.

## 16. What happens if I get it wrong?

Today: the Authority can investigate a complaint and issue a binding directive to stop, fix, or compensate (s.35 — in force). Fines of up to Rs 10 million per non-compliance (s.38) are for ignoring such a directive and are in **Part VII, not yet in force**. Directors and partners can be personally liable when it is.

## 17. Timeline for a small business

| When | What applies |
|---|---|
| Now | The Authority exists and can investigate complaints and issue directives. No Part I/III duties yet. |
| 1 Jan 2027 | Lawful basis, purpose, minimisation, accuracy, retention, security, notice, DPMP (Part I); processor contracts, breach notification, DPIAs, cross-border rules (Part III). |
| No date yet | Customer rights and one-month responses (Part II); consent-only marketing (Part IV); fines (Part VII). |

## 1. What the law says (referenced extract)

> Processing is lawful only if the controller complies with (a) a condition in Schedule I (general lawful bases); (b) a Schedule II condition for *special categories* of personal data; (c) *all* the Schedule III conditions where processing relies on consent; or (d) *all* the Schedule IV conditions for criminal-investigation processing. — s.5

**Schedule I (any one):** (a) consent; (b) necessary for a contract with the data subject or pre-contract steps at their request; (c) necessary to comply with a legal obligation under written law; (d) necessary to respond to an emergency threatening life, health or safety; (e) necessary for a task in the public interest or the exercise of powers/duties under written law (incl. government circulars, directions, codes); (f) necessary for the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party, unless overridden by the data subject's interests, "in particular where the data subject is a child". Sch I(h) says legitimate interests include situations where the data subject is a client, where processing is reasonably expected, fraud prevention, and network/information security.

**Schedule II (special categories, any one):** consent (parent/guardian for a child); employment, social security or public-health purposes provided for by written law; emergency where the person cannot consent; data manifestly made public by the data subject; legal claims; written law or public interest with safeguards; health care by a licensed health professional; archiving/research/statistics in accordance with law.

**Schedule III (consent, all):** controller must be able to demonstrate consent; a consent request must be clearly distinguishable, intelligible and in plain language; whether consent is "freely given" takes special account of whether a service is made conditional on unnecessary consent; the person must be told in advance that consent can be withdrawn.

## 2. In plain English

You need a proper legal reason for every use of personal information. Consent is only one of six reasons — for most ordinary business uses, 'it's necessary for the contract' (selling the goods, delivering the order, employing the person) or 'legitimate interests' will be the right reason, and asking for consent you don't need can backfire because consent must be freely given and can be withdrawn. Sensitive information (health, religion, ethnicity, criminal records, biometrics, and **anything about a child under 16**) needs a stronger reason from a shorter list.

## 4. What it means in practice

For each type of information you hold, be able to say which Schedule I condition you rely on. If you hold any special-category data, you also need a Schedule II condition. If you rely on consent, the consent must meet all four Schedule III tests — a pre-ticked box or 'by using this service you agree' will not do. Under the Act, consent is 'any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication' by written declaration or affirmative action (s.56).

## 5. Who is affected

Every controller. Particularly important for: anyone handling children's data (schools, tuition classes, paediatric clinics, kids' sports), health data (hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, insurers, employers' medical records), biometrics (fingerprint attendance, facial recognition), and marketing lists.

## 6. Everyday example

A restaurant that takes a customer's name and phone number for a table reservation relies on Sch I(b) — it is necessary to perform the service the customer asked for. If the same restaurant wants to send that customer birthday offers, that is a different purpose; it will need consent (and Part IV, when in force, separately requires consent for marketing messages — see s.27).

## 7. What to consider doing

- List what personal data you hold and why; write the Schedule I (and, if relevant, Schedule II) condition next to each.
- Stop collecting data you have no condition for.
- Where you rely on consent, redesign the consent so it meets Schedule III (separate, plain, unbundled, withdrawable) and keep a record of it.
- Identify any data about children under 16 and any special-category data; apply Schedule II.

## 8. Consequences of not complying

A failure to meet this obligation is a contravention of the Act. The Authority may inquire (s.35, in force) and issue a binding directive requiring the controller to stop, rectify or pay compensation (s.35(2)). Administrative penalties of up to Rs 10 million per non-compliance with a directive (s.38) sit in Part VII, which is **not yet in force**. A data subject's right to complain or appeal under Part II is also not yet in force.

## 9. Current legal status

**In force from 1 January 2027.** Appointed by Gazette Extraordinary No. 2498/16 of 22 July 2026 (Order under s.1(3) as substituted by Act No. 22 of 2025). Until that date there is no legal duty under this section. Note that Part II (rights of individuals) and Part VII (penalties) are **not** in force and have no appointed date, so from 1 January 2027 this duty exists in law but cannot yet be enforced through the Part VII penalty process; the Authority's Part V powers (inquiries and directives under s.35) are already in operation.

# Section 11 — Transparency — information to be given to people

Part I: Processing of Personal Data (ss.4–12) · Status: **In force from 1 January 2027** (Gazette 2498/16)

## 1. What the law says (referenced extract)

> A controller shall provide data subjects with the information in Schedule V, and information about decisions on Part II requests, "in writing or by electronic means and in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form". — s.11

**Schedule V item 1** (when data is collected from the person): identity and contact details of the controller; DPO contact details where applicable; purposes and legal basis; the legitimate interest relied on (if any); categories of data; the right to withdraw consent and how; recipients; any cross-border transfer; retention period or criteria; the existence of and procedure for Part II rights; the right to complain to the Authority; whether providing the data is a statutory/contractual requirement and the consequences of not providing it; the existence of automated decision-making incl. profiling with meaningful information about the logic. Item 2: new purposes must be notified. Items 4–5: where data is obtained indirectly, the source must be given, within one month at the latest. Item 6: exceptions (already informed; impossible/disproportionate effort; laid down by law; professional secrecy).

## 2. In plain English

Tell people clearly, at the time you collect their information, who you are, why you want it, what you will do with it, how long you'll keep it, who you'll share it with, and what their rights are. In practice this is a privacy notice — on your website, a sign at the counter, a line on the form, or a message when someone signs up.

## 4. What it means in practice

The notice must be in writing or electronic — a verbal explanation is not enough. It must be concise and intelligible, which for a public-facing Sri Lankan business means Sinhala and/or Tamil as well as English. Items referring to Part II rights and complaints to the Authority can be included now with a note that those provisions are not yet in force.

## 5. Who is affected

Every controller that collects personal data, directly or indirectly.

## 6. Everyday example

A clothing shop asks customers for a phone number at the till 'for the loyalty programme'. Schedule V requires the shop to tell them (in writing — a counter card or a message is fine) who the shop is, that the number is used for loyalty points, whether it will be used for promotions, how long it is kept, and that they can ask about their data.

## 7. What to consider doing

- Draft a short privacy notice from the Schedule V list (a template will be provided in the Small Business Guide).
- Put it where people give you data: counter, form, website, app, WhatsApp auto-reply.
- Translate it; keep it short.

## 8. Consequences of not complying

A failure to meet this obligation is a contravention of the Act. The Authority may inquire (s.35, in force) and issue a binding directive requiring the controller to stop, rectify or pay compensation (s.35(2)). Administrative penalties of up to Rs 10 million per non-compliance with a directive (s.38) sit in Part VII, which is **not yet in force**. A data subject's right to complain or appeal under Part II is also not yet in force.

## 9. Current legal status

**In force from 1 January 2027.** Appointed by Gazette Extraordinary No. 2498/16 of 22 July 2026 (Order under s.1(3) as substituted by Act No. 22 of 2025). Until that date there is no legal duty under this section. Note that Part II (rights of individuals) and Part VII (penalties) are **not** in force and have no appointed date, so from 1 January 2027 this duty exists in law but cannot yet be enforced through the Part VII penalty process; the Authority's Part V powers (inquiries and directives under s.35) are already in operation.

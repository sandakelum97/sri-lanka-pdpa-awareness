## 1. What the law says (referenced extract)

> Before processing that involves (a) "a systematic and extensive evaluation of personal data or special categories of personal data including profiling"; (b) "a systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas or telecommunication networks"; or (c) activities determined by rules, a controller shall carry out a personal data protection impact assessment "in a form and manner as may be prescribed" to assess the impact on Part I obligations and Part II rights. — s.24(1). It must record mitigating measures (24(2)); the controller shall seek the assistance of the DPO (24(3), as amended); a fresh DPIA is needed when methodology, technology or process changes (24(4)); the DPIA must be submitted to the Authority **upon its written request** (24(5), as replaced).

## 2. In plain English

Before you start any large-scale profiling, or any systematic monitoring of public places or communication networks — CCTV networks are the obvious example — you must do a written risk assessment first, keep it, update it when things change, and give it to the Authority if asked.

## 4. What it means in practice

The form of a DPIA is 'as may be prescribed' and no regulations exist; the 2024 draft (DRF-01) shows what the DPA has in mind (a template and a 5×5 risk matrix) but is not law. The 2025 amendment removed the duty to file every DPIA automatically — now only on the Authority's request. Whether a single shop's two-camera CCTV is 'systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas' is an open question of interpretation; a city-wide or mall-wide system clearly is.

## 5. Who is affected

Controllers doing profiling at scale (credit scoring, ad-tech, HR analytics), operators of CCTV networks and smart-city systems, telecom and ISP monitoring, large biometric deployments, public authorities (see also s.25(7)).

## 6. Everyday example

A shopping mall installs 200 cameras with facial recognition to track visitors. Before switching on, s.24(1)(a) and (b) both require a DPIA; the DPO must assist; the Authority may call for it.

## 7. What to consider doing

- Identify processing that falls under s.24(1)(a) or (b).
- Do a written assessment now using the DRF-01 structure as a guide (labelled as such), to be re-cut when the form is prescribed.
- Re-do it on every significant change.

## 8. Consequences of not complying

A failure to meet this obligation is a contravention of the Act. The Authority may inquire (s.35, in force) and issue a binding directive requiring the controller to stop, rectify or pay compensation (s.35(2)). Administrative penalties of up to Rs 10 million per non-compliance with a directive (s.38) sit in Part VII, which is **not yet in force**. A data subject's right to complain or appeal under Part II is also not yet in force.

## 9. Current legal status

**In force from 1 January 2027.** Appointed by Gazette Extraordinary No. 2498/16 of 22 July 2026 (Order under s.1(3) as substituted by Act No. 22 of 2025). Until that date there is no legal duty under this section. Note that Part II (rights of individuals) and Part VII (penalties) are **not** in force and have no appointed date, so from 1 January 2027 this duty exists in law but cannot yet be enforced through the Part VII penalty process; the Authority's Part V powers (inquiries and directives under s.35) are already in operation.

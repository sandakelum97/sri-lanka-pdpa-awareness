# Section 26 — Cross-border data flows

Part III: Controllers and Processors (ss.20–26) · Status: **In force from 1 January 2027** (Gazette 2498/16)

## 1. What the law says (referenced extract)

> (1) A controller or processor may engage in cross-border data flows only where it ensures compliance with Part I, Part II and ss.20–25. (2) To ensure that compliance it shall adopt "such instruments as may be specified by a directive issued by the Authority under paragraph (c) of section 33" to secure binding and enforceable commitments from the recipient in the third country. (3) Notwithstanding (1), transfers are permitted where: (a) the data subject has explicitly consented after being informed of the possible risks; (b) necessary for a contract with the data subject or pre-contract steps at their request; (c) necessary for legal claims; (d) necessary for public interest as defined in Sch I(g); (e) necessary for an emergency where the person cannot consent; (f) the transfer is only a transit through Sri Lanka; or (g) other prescribed conditions. (4)–(5) A public authority shall not transfer categories of personal data the Minister prescribes. — s.26 as replaced by Act No. 22 of 2025. 'Third country' now means any territory or country other than Sri Lanka (s.56 as amended).

## 2. In plain English

Sending personal data abroad — which includes storing it on a cloud service or using a messaging app whose servers are overseas — is allowed, but you must keep meeting the Act's obligations for that data and use safeguards the Authority will specify in a directive (not yet issued). Separately, transfers are allowed with the person's explicit informed consent, or when needed for a contract with them, legal claims, public interest, emergencies, or mere transit. Government bodies will be barred from sending abroad whatever categories the Minister lists (none listed yet).

## 4. What it means in practice

This is the section most changed by the 2025 amendment. The old adequacy-decision model and the ban on public authorities processing abroad are gone; the DPA describes the change as enabling a 'cloud-first' approach for both sectors (Circular 01/2026 para 4.6). The practical gap: the s.26(2) instruments directive does not exist (the 2024 draft, DRF-03, listed binding corporate rules, contracts, codes, certifications, impact assessments and board resolutions — a draft only). From 1 January 2027, a business using foreign cloud or SaaS should (i) rely on s.26(3)(b) where the transfer is genuinely necessary for the customer contract, (ii) otherwise obtain explicit informed consent under s.26(3)(a), and (iii) have contractual data-protection commitments from the provider so it is ready for the directive.

## 5. Who is affected

Practically every business using Gmail/Microsoft 365/Google Workspace, WhatsApp Business, Shopify, Zoho, Salesforce, AWS/Azure, foreign payroll or CRM tools; BPOs serving foreign clients; public authorities.

## 6. Everyday example

A Colombo accounting firm stores client files in Microsoft 365 hosted outside Sri Lanka. That is a cross-border data flow. The firm must keep complying with Parts I–III for that data, hold Microsoft's data-protection commitments, and — once the directive issues — use the specified instrument. Where storage abroad is necessary to deliver the service the client contracted for, s.26(3)(b) also applies.

## 7. What to consider doing

- Inventory which systems hold personal data outside Sri Lanka.
- Check each provider's data-processing terms and where data is hosted.
- Add a cross-border line to your privacy notice (Schedule V item 1(h)).
- Watch for the s.26(2) directive and, for public authorities, the Minister's prescribed categories.

## 8. Consequences of not complying

A failure to meet this obligation is a contravention of the Act. The Authority may inquire (s.35, in force) and issue a binding directive requiring the controller to stop, rectify or pay compensation (s.35(2)). Administrative penalties of up to Rs 10 million per non-compliance with a directive (s.38) sit in Part VII, which is **not yet in force**. A data subject's right to complain or appeal under Part II is also not yet in force.

## 9. Current legal status

**In force from 1 January 2027.** Appointed by Gazette Extraordinary No. 2498/16 of 22 July 2026 (Order under s.1(3) as substituted by Act No. 22 of 2025). Until that date there is no legal duty under this section. Note that Part II (rights of individuals) and Part VII (penalties) are **not** in force and have no appointed date, so from 1 January 2027 this duty exists in law but cannot yet be enforced through the Part VII penalty process; the Authority's Part V powers (inquiries and directives under s.35) are already in operation.
